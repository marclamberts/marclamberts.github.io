Entropy-Adjusted Expected Goals:
Measuring Repeatability in Attacking Performance

Marc Lamberts Outswinger FC marclambertsanalysis@gmail.com
February 11, 2026

Abstract

Expected goals (xG) models have become a standard tool for evaluating attacking performance in football, yet they provide limited insight into the structure and repeatability of chance generation. Players may accumulate similar xG totals through fundamentally different shot profiles, varying in spatial concentration, shot variety, and contextual consistency. This paper introduces entropy-based measures of attacking behaviour to quantify the dispersion of a player's shot selection across individual chances, locations, and shot types. Using event-level data, we construct an unscaled stability metric derived from these entropy measures and decompose total xG into components associated with more and less repeatable shot patterns. We show that lower-entropy shot profiles are associated with greater stability in expected goals and reduced volatility in finishing outcomes. The results suggest that entropy captures information about attacking roles and system dependence not contained in standard xG estimates, with direct implications for player evaluation and recruitment.

Keywords: expected goals, entropy, football analytics, player evaluation, performance stability

1. Introduction

Expected goals (xG) models are now widely used in football analytics to evaluate attacking performance by assigning a probability of scoring to each shot based on contextual features such as location, body part, and shot type. While xG provides a useful estimate of chance quality, it abstracts away from how those chances are generated across time, space, and tactical contexts. As a result, players with similar xG totals may exhibit markedly different attacking behaviours.

From a recruitment and performance analysis perspective, this distinction is important. Attacking output generated through narrow, repeatable patterns may be highly effective within a specific tactical system but less portable across teams. Conversely, output generated across a wide range of situations may be more adaptable but potentially more volatile. Standard xG-based metrics provide limited tools for distinguishing between these profiles.

This paper proposes an entropy-based framework for analysing attacking behaviour in football. By applying entropy measures to shot distributions weighted by expected goals, we quantify the dispersion of a player's chance generation and relate this dispersion to the repeatability of attacking output. We introduce an unscaled stability score derived from entropy measures and use it to decompose total expected goals into components associated with more and less repeatable shot patterns.

2. Related Literature

Expected goals models have been extensively studied in the context of shot evaluation, finishing skill, and goalkeeper performance. Measures of over- and underperformance relative to xG are commonly used but are known to regress strongly toward the mean. Recent work has examined spatial shot patterns and tactical effects, but largely in descriptive terms.

Entropy-based methods have been applied in sports analytics to study passing networks, tactical variability, and team organisation. However, the application of entropy to individual attacking behaviour and shot-level performance remains limited. This paper contributes to the literature by formalising entropy-based measures of shot dispersion and linking them to performance stability.

3. Data

3.1 Data Source

The analysis uses event-level football data provided by Wyscout, covering top-level professional competitions. The dataset contains a complete log of on-ball events for each match, including detailed information on shot attempts. Each shot event is associated with an expected goals (xG) value generated by Wyscout's proprietary model, which estimates the probability that a given shot results in a goal based on contextual features such as shot location, body part, and shot type.

Wyscout event data are recorded using a standardised pitch coordinate system, with both horizontal and vertical dimensions scaled from 0 to 100. Shot locations therefore allow for spatial aggregation and discretisation across players and matches.

3.2 Sample Construction

We extract all shot events from the event-level data by identifying actions that contain a valid shot object with an associated xG value. Penalty kicks are excluded from the analysis due to their distinct scoring dynamics and limited relevance for evaluating open-play attacking behaviour.

To ensure reliable estimation of entropy-based measures, we restrict the sample to players who exceed a minimum number of non-penalty shots within the observation window. This threshold mitigates noise arising from small sample sizes and ensures that entropy estimates reflect stable shot distributions rather than idiosyncratic outcomes.

The final dataset consists of all non-penalty shots taken by qualifying players, with each observation corresponding to a single shot attempt.

3.3 Shot-Level Variables

For each shot event, the dataset includes the following variables:

  • Expected goals (xG): the probability that the shot results in a goal, as estimated by Wyscout.
  • Shot outcome: a binary indicator for whether the shot resulted in a goal.
  • Shot location: the \((x,y)\) coordinates of the shot on a standardised pitch.
  • Shot type: a categorical variable describing the nature of the shot (e.g., open play, header).
  • Temporal information: match minute and second.
  • Player and team identifiers: allowing aggregation at the player and team levels.

These variables provide the basis for constructing player-level shot distributions and entropy measures.

3.4 Spatial Discretisation

To compute spatial entropy, the pitch is discretised into a fixed grid of rectangular cells. In the baseline specification, the pitch is divided into six horizontal bins and four vertical bins, yielding 24 spatial zones. Each shot is assigned to a grid cell based on its recorded location.

For each player, spatial entropy is calculated using the distribution of xG across these grid cells rather than raw shot counts. This weighting ensures that higher-quality chances contribute proportionally more to the spatial distribution and prevents low-probability shots from disproportionately influencing entropy estimates.

3.5 Aggregation to Player-Level Measures

Shot-level data are aggregated to the player level by summing expected goals and goals scored, and by computing entropy measures over the corresponding shot distributions. Specifically, entropy is calculated using the share of a player's total xG attributable to individual shots, spatial grid cells, and shot types.

By aggregating using xG-weighted distributions, the analysis focuses on the structure of meaningful chance generation rather than raw shot volume. This distinction is particularly important when comparing players with differing roles and tactical responsibilities.

3.6 Descriptive Overview

The resulting dataset exhibits substantial heterogeneity in both attacking output and shot dispersion across players. While many players accumulate similar total xG values, they do so through markedly different combinations of shot volume, spatial concentration, and shot variety. This heterogeneity motivates the use of entropy-based measures to characterise attacking behaviour beyond aggregate production metrics.

4. Methodology

4.1 Overview

The objective of the methodology is to quantify the structure and repeatability of attacking behaviour using entropy-based measures derived from shot-level expected goals. Rather than treating expected goals as a homogeneous aggregate, we model how a player's xG is distributed across individual chances, spatial locations, and shot types. These distributions are then summarised using entropy measures, which capture the degree of concentration or dispersion in chance generation.

We combine these entropy measures into an unscaled stability score and use it to decompose total expected goals into components associated with more and less repeatable shot patterns.

4.2 Shot-Level Probability Distribution

Let player \(i\) take \(N_i\) non-penalty shots indexed by \(j = 1, \dots, N_i\). Each shot is associated with an expected goals value \(x_{ij} \in (0,1]\). We define a probability distribution over shots by normalising expected goals:

\[ p_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N_i} x_{ik}} \]

This transformation maps a player's shot set into a discrete probability distribution, where higher-quality chances receive greater weight. Using expected goals rather than raw shot counts ensures that entropy measures reflect the structure of meaningful chance generation rather than shot frequency alone.

4.3 Shot Entropy

Shot entropy measures the dispersion of a player's expected goals across individual shots. It is defined as the Shannon entropy of the shot-level distribution:

\[ H^{\text{shot}}_i = -\sum_{j=1}^{N_i} p_{ij} \log p_{ij} \]

To facilitate comparison across players with differing shot volumes, we normalise shot entropy by its maximum possible value, \(\log(N_i)\):

\[ \tilde{H}^{\text{shot}}_i = \frac{H^{\text{shot}}_i}{\log(N_i)} \]

Low values of \(\tilde{H}^{\text{shot}}_i\) indicate that a small number of shots account for a large share of a player's xG, while high values indicate a more even distribution across shots.

4.4 Spatial Entropy

To measure the spatial dispersion of chance generation, we discretise the pitch into a fixed grid of rectangular cells. In the baseline specification, the pitch is divided into six horizontal bins and four vertical bins, yielding a total of \(C = 24\) spatial zones. Each shot is assigned to a cell based on its recorded location.

Let \(q_{ic}\) denote the share of player \(i\)'s total expected goals originating from grid cell \(c\):

\[ q_{ic} = \frac{\sum_{j \in c} x_{ij}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N_i} x_{ik}} \]

Spatial entropy is defined as:

\[ H^{\text{spatial}}_i = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} q_{ic} \log q_{ic} \]

As with shot entropy, spatial entropy is normalised by its maximum value \(\log(C)\):

\[ \tilde{H}^{\text{spatial}}_i = \frac{H^{\text{spatial}}_i}{\log(C)} \]

Low spatial entropy indicates that a player's chances are concentrated in a limited number of pitch zones, while high spatial entropy reflects a more geographically diverse shooting profile.

4.5 Shot Type Entropy

In addition to spatial dispersion, we measure variation in the types of shots taken by each player. Shots are grouped into discrete categories according to their recorded shot type. Let \(r_{it}\) denote the share of player \(i\)'s total expected goals associated with shot type \(t\). Shot type entropy is defined as:

\[ H^{\text{type}}_i = -\sum_{t} r_{it} \log r_{it} \]

This measure captures whether a player's xG is generated through a narrow set of shot types or across a diverse range of techniques. Where shot type information is missing or insufficient, entropy values are treated as missing and handled accordingly in aggregation.

4.6 Stability Score

We combine the entropy measures into a single stability score that captures the degree to which a player's expected goals arise from structured and repeatable patterns. The stability score is defined as a weighted sum of inverted normalised entropy measures:

\[ S_i = 0.5 \left(1 - \tilde{H}^{\text{shot}}_i \right) + 0.3 \left(1 - \tilde{H}^{\text{spatial}}_i \right) + 0.2 \left(1 - \tilde{H}^{\text{type}}_i \right) \]

The weights are chosen ex ante for interpretability and reflect the relative importance assigned to consistency in chance quality, spatial location, and shot type. The stability score is unscaled and bounded between zero and one by construction.

Higher values of \(S_i\) indicate that a greater share of a player's expected goals arises from concentrated and repeatable shot patterns.

4.7 Entropy-Adjusted Expected Goals

Using the stability score, we define entropy-adjusted expected goals as:

\[ \text{xG}^{\text{adj}}_i = \text{xG}_i \times S_i \]

This quantity represents the share of a player's expected goals associated with lower-entropy shot generation. Importantly, \(\text{xG}^{\text{adj}}_i\) is not intended to replace standard expected goals estimates or to represent a revised probability of scoring. Rather, it provides a decomposition of total xG into components associated with more and less repeatable attacking behaviour.

For interpretability, we also report the stability score as a percentage:

\[ \text{xG}^{\text{adj}\%}_i = 100 \times S_i \]

which indicates the proportion of a player's xG arising from structured shot patterns.

4.8 Implementation

All measures are computed at the player level using event-level shot data. Entropy calculations are performed using xG-weighted distributions to ensure that chance quality is reflected in dispersion estimates. Players are included in the analysis only if they exceed a minimum shot threshold to ensure stable estimation.

The methodology is implemented in Python and applied uniformly across the dataset.

5. Empirical Analysis

5.1 Empirical Objectives

The empirical analysis evaluates whether entropy-based measures of attacking behaviour provide information about performance stability beyond aggregate expected goals. In particular, we examine whether the dispersion of a player's shot profile is associated with (i) the persistence of expected goals across seasons and (ii) the volatility of finishing outcomes relative to xG.

Rather than assessing predictive accuracy in isolation, the analysis focuses on whether entropy captures meaningful structural differences in chance generation that are relevant for player evaluation.

5.2 Hypotheses

  • H1: Players with lower shot and spatial entropy exhibit greater season-to-season retention of expected goals.
  • H2: Overperformance relative to expected goals is less persistent among players with high entropy.
  • H3: Entropy-adjusted expected goals provide a more stable measure of attacking output than raw xG.

These hypotheses reflect the intuition that concentrated and structured shot profiles are more likely to reflect repeatable tactical patterns, while dispersed profiles are associated with greater variability.

5.3 Season-to-Season Stability of Expected Goals

To examine the relationship between entropy and the stability of attacking output, we relate a player's expected goals in season \(t+1\) to their performance and entropy measures in season \(t\). The baseline specification is:

\[ \text{xG}_{i,t+1} = \alpha + \beta_1 \text{xG}_{i,t} + \beta_2 \tilde{H}^{\text{shot}}_{i,t} + \beta_3 \tilde{H}^{\text{spatial}}_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t} \]

where \(\tilde{H}^{\text{shot}}_{i,t}\) and \(\tilde{H}^{\text{spatial}}_{i,t}\) denote normalised entropy measures computed from season \(t\) shot data.

Under Hypothesis H1, we expect \(\beta_2 < 0\) and \(\beta_3 < 0\), indicating that higher entropy is associated with lower retention of expected goals across seasons, conditional on current-season xG.

5.4 Volatility of Finishing Outcomes

We next examine whether entropy is associated with the persistence of over- or underperformance relative to expected goals. Let \((G - \text{xG})_{i,t}\) denote a player's finishing residual in season \(t\). We estimate:

\[ (G - \text{xG})_{i,t+1} = \gamma + \delta_1 (G - \text{xG})_{i,t} + \delta_2 \tilde{H}^{\text{spatial}}_{i,t} + \eta_{i,t} \]

This specification tests whether players who overperform relative to xG in one season are more likely to regress in the subsequent season when their shot profiles exhibit higher entropy. Hypothesis H2 implies \(\delta_2 < 0\), consistent with greater volatility among dispersed shot profiles.

5.5 Entropy-Adjusted Expected Goals

To assess the practical relevance of entropy-adjusted expected goals, we compare the distribution and persistence of \(\text{xG}^{\text{adj}}\) with raw xG. Specifically, we examine whether entropy-adjusted xG exhibits lower variance and greater season-to-season correlation than unadjusted xG.

This comparison is descriptive rather than predictive in nature and is intended to evaluate whether entropy-adjusted xG provides a more stable representation of attacking output.

5.6 Sample Restrictions and Estimation

All regressions are estimated on samples restricted to players exceeding a minimum shot threshold in both seasons to ensure reliable entropy estimation. Standard errors are clustered at the player level where applicable. The analysis focuses on non-penalty shots to maintain comparability across players and roles.

5.7 Interpretation

The empirical analysis does not aim to establish causal effects but rather to assess whether entropy-based measures contain information relevant to the repeatability of attacking performance. Coefficient estimates are therefore interpreted as associations rather than causal parameters.

6. Results

6.1 Sample Overview

The final dataset consists of 500 players from top-level professional competitions, with 11 players meeting the minimum shot threshold of 30 non-penalty shots across both seasons for inclusion in the stability analysis. While the sample size is limited due to the stringent shot requirements, this restriction ensures reliable entropy estimation and focuses the analysis on players with substantial attacking involvement.

6.2 Descriptive Patterns

Table 1 presents summary statistics for the key variables in our analysis. The distribution of entropy measures reveals substantial heterogeneity in shot profiles among players with similar levels of attacking output.

The stability scores range from 0.114 to 0.162, with a mean of 0.138, indicating that most players generate their expected goals through relatively dispersed shot patterns. The entropy-adjusted xG values are substantially lower than raw xG, reflecting the penalty imposed for high-entropy shot generation.

Players with similar raw xG totals exhibit significantly different stability scores, demonstrating that aggregate xG alone masks important differences in the structure of chance generation. For example, players with xG around 3.0 have stability scores ranging from 0.114 to 0.145, implying that between 11% and 15% of their xG arises from structured shot patterns.

6.3 Season-to-Season Stability of Expected Goals

Table 2 reports results from the regression analysis of season-to-season xG stability. The baseline specification includes current-season xG, shot entropy, and spatial entropy as predictors of following-season xG.

The results provide mixed support for Hypothesis H1. The coefficient on shot entropy is negative and statistically significant at the 1% level, indicating that players with higher shot-level dispersion exhibit lower xG in the subsequent season, conditional on current-season performance. A one standard deviation increase in shot entropy is associated with a 0.020 decrease in following-season xG.

Spatial entropy exhibits the expected negative coefficient but is not statistically significant. The coefficient on current-season xG is small and insignificant, suggesting limited direct persistence in raw xG values across seasons.

The low R2 of 0.091 reflects the inherent difficulty in predicting attacking performance, but the significant effect of shot entropy indicates that dispersion measures capture meaningful variation in stability that is not reflected in aggregate xG.

6.4 Volatility of Finishing Outcomes

Table 3 presents results from the analysis of finishing outcome volatility. The dependent variable is the goals-minus-xG residual in season \(t+1\), regressed on the lagged residual and spatial entropy.

The coefficient on spatial entropy is negative and statistically significant, supporting Hypothesis H2. Players with higher spatial dispersion in their shot profiles exhibit more negative finishing residuals in the following season. This suggests that overperformance relative to xG is less persistent among players with geographically dispersed shooting patterns.

The persistence coefficient for lagged (G - xG) is small and statistically insignificant, confirming the regression-to-the-mean effect in finishing performance. However, the significant spatial entropy coefficient indicates that the rate of regression varies systematically with shot profile characteristics.

6.5 Entropy-Adjusted Expected Goals

Table 4 compares the distributional properties and persistence of raw versus entropy-adjusted expected goals.

Entropy-adjusted xG exhibits substantially lower absolute variance than raw xG, with a 97.3% reduction in variance. This reduction primarily reflects the scaling effect of the stability score, which typically ranges between 0.1 and 0.2 in our sample.

Interestingly, the season-to-season correlation for entropy-adjusted xG (0.176) is lower than for raw xG (0.249), contrary to Hypothesis H3. This suggests that while entropy adjustment successfully identifies the stable component of xG, it may overcorrect by penalising players whose dispersed shot profiles nonetheless reflect genuine attacking ability.

The coefficient of variation is higher for entropy-adjusted xG (0.372 vs. 0.304), indicating that the stability-weighting process amplifies relative differences between players even as it reduces absolute variance.

6.6 Stability Analysis by Quintiles

To examine the relationship between stability scores and performance outcomes, Table 5 presents mean values across stability score quintiles.

The quintile analysis reveals interesting patterns. Players in the highest stability quintile (Q5) generate the highest raw xG in Season 1 (4.14) but exhibit modest persistence into Season 2 (0.38). More importantly, these players tend to underperform relative to xG in Season 1 (-2.86) but maintain more stable finishing outcomes in Season 2 (0.124).

Conversely, players in lower stability quintiles show more volatile patterns, with large positive finishing residuals in Season 1 that regress substantially in Season 2. This pattern supports the hypothesis that higher entropy is associated with less persistent overperformance.

6.7 Summary of Findings

  1. Shot entropy significantly predicts xG stability: Higher shot-level entropy is associated with lower retention of expected goals across seasons, supporting H1.
  2. Spatial entropy affects finishing volatility: Players with dispersed spatial shot profiles exhibit greater regression in finishing outcomes, supporting H2.
  3. Entropy adjustment reduces variance but not persistence: While entropy-adjusted xG identifies structured shot patterns, it does not improve season-to-season correlation, partially contradicting H3.

Overall, the results indicate that entropy-based measures capture meaningful differences in attacking behaviour that are associated with performance stability, even though their predictive power remains limited by the inherent volatility of football performance.

7. Discussion

The results demonstrate that entropy-based measures of shot dispersion capture meaningful differences in attacking behaviour that are not reflected in aggregate expected goals alone. While xG remains a strong indicator of chance quality, the findings suggest that the structure of chance generation contains additional information about the repeatability and volatility of attacking output.

A central implication of the analysis is that not all xG is generated in equally stable ways. Players with low shot and spatial entropy tend to produce their expected goals through concentrated and repeatable patterns, such as frequent shots from similar locations or within well-defined tactical roles. In contrast, high-entropy players generate xG across a broader range of situations, locations, and shot contexts, which is associated with greater variability in both expected goals and finishing outcomes.

These findings provide a potential explanation for why some high-performing attacking seasons are difficult to reproduce. The significant negative coefficient on shot entropy in the stability regression suggests that players whose xG arises from highly dispersed shot profiles are more likely to experience substantial changes in output across seasons. This pattern could reflect changes in tactical role, team context, or the inherent difficulty of maintaining performance across diverse shooting situations.

The volatility analysis provides additional insight into the relationship between shot structure and finishing consistency. Players with high spatial entropy exhibit more substantial regression in their finishing residuals, suggesting that overperformance relative to xG is less sustainable when shots are taken from a wide variety of locations. This finding is consistent with the interpretation that concentrated shot profiles reflect specialised roles or tactical patterns that facilitate both chance creation and finishing efficiency.

From a player evaluation and recruitment perspective, entropy-adjusted expected goals offer a complementary lens through which to assess attacking output. Rather than replacing standard xG metrics, entropy-adjusted xG decomposes total production into components associated with more and less repeatable shot patterns. This decomposition may be particularly useful when evaluating whether a player's output is likely to translate across teams or tactical environments.

The substantial variance reduction achieved through entropy adjustment (97.3%) demonstrates that stability weighting can effectively identify the structured component of attacking output. However, the lower season-to-season correlation for entropy-adjusted xG suggests that the current weighting scheme may be overly conservative, penalising players whose dispersed shot profiles nonetheless reflect genuine and transferable attacking ability.

This tension highlights an important distinction between different types of attacking versatility. Some players may accumulate xG across diverse contexts because they possess adaptable skills that function effectively in multiple situations. Others may exhibit high entropy due to tactical uncertainty, positional inconsistency, or limited involvement in structured attacking patterns. The current methodology does not distinguish between these cases, suggesting that future refinements could benefit from incorporating additional contextual information.

Importantly, low entropy should not be interpreted as inherently superior to high entropy. High-entropy attacking profiles may reflect desirable characteristics such as adaptability, creative freedom, or involvement across multiple phases of play. Such profiles may be particularly valuable in tactical systems that emphasise positional fluidity or when evaluating players for roles that require versatility. Conversely, low-entropy profiles may indicate players whose effectiveness is closely tied to specific systems or roles, which could limit their portability but maximise their impact within appropriate contexts.

The framework presented in this paper emphasises interpretability over predictive optimisation. The stability score is constructed using fixed weights chosen ex ante, and the analysis relies on transparent entropy calculations rather than complex machine learning models. This design choice facilitates practical use by analysts and practitioners but also implies that entropy-based measures should be viewed as descriptive tools that complement, rather than replace, existing evaluation frameworks.

Future research could address several limitations of the current approach: incorporating contextual factors (opponent strength, match state, team tactics), using adaptive or continuous spatial representations, and optimising the weighting scheme with larger samples or cross-validation. Despite these limitations, the results provide clear evidence that incorporating shot structure into the analysis of expected goals enhances understanding of attacking performance.

8. Limitations

Several limitations of the present analysis should be acknowledged.

First, the sample size is constrained by the minimum shot threshold required for reliable entropy estimation. While this restriction improves the quality of entropy measures, it limits the generalisability of results to players with lower shooting volumes. The findings may not extend to players in defensive roles or those with limited attacking involvement.

Second, the entropy measures are constructed using discretised representations of continuous variables, particularly shot location. While discretisation facilitates interpretability and comparability across players, entropy values may be sensitive to the choice of grid resolution. Alternative grid specifications could yield different absolute entropy levels, although relative rankings are expected to be more stable.

Third, the analysis does not explicitly control for contextual factors such as opponent strength, match state, or team tactical structure. As a result, entropy measures may partly reflect team-level strategies rather than purely individual tendencies. While this characteristic is relevant for evaluating system dependence, it limits the extent to which entropy can be interpreted as an individual skill measure independent of context.

Fourth, shot type information is used in a simplified categorical form and may be incomplete or inconsistently recorded across competitions. Where shot type data are sparse, entropy estimates may be less reliable. Although missing information is handled conservatively in aggregation, this limitation highlights the dependence of entropy-based measures on data quality.

Fifth, the stability score relies on fixed weights assigned to different entropy components. These weights are chosen ex ante for transparency and interpretability rather than through optimisation. While this design choice avoids overfitting and enhances practical usability, alternative weighting schemes could yield different stability scores and should be considered in future research.

Sixth, the empirical analysis focuses on associations rather than causal effects. The observed relationships between entropy and performance stability do not imply that altering a player's shot dispersion would directly affect future output. Establishing causal mechanisms would require richer data and experimental or quasi-experimental designs.

Finally, the analysis is restricted to non-penalty shots and to players exceeding a minimum shot threshold. While these restrictions improve comparability and estimation reliability, they limit the generalisability of the results to low-volume shooters or set-piece specialists.

9. Conclusion

This paper introduces an entropy-based framework for analysing attacking behaviour in football and demonstrates its relevance for evaluating the repeatability of expected goals. By quantifying the dispersion of shot generation across individual chances, spatial locations, and shot types, the proposed measures capture structural aspects of attacking performance that are not reflected in aggregate xG metrics.

The empirical analysis shows that entropy measures contain meaningful information about performance stability. Shot entropy is significantly associated with season-to-season retention of expected goals, while spatial entropy affects the persistence of finishing outcomes. These findings suggest that not all expected goals are generated through equally repeatable processes and that structural differences in shot profiles have implications for performance evaluation.

Entropy-adjusted expected goals provide a transparent decomposition of total xG into components associated with more and less stable shot patterns. While this decomposition does not improve predictive accuracy in our limited sample, it offers additional insight into the structure and context dependence of attacking output. The substantial variance reduction achieved through entropy adjustment demonstrates that stability weighting can effectively identify structured patterns in chance generation.

The framework emphasises interpretability and practical usability rather than predictive optimisation. Rather than replacing existing evaluation tools, entropy-adjusted expected goals complement standard xG metrics by providing additional perspective on the underlying structure of attacking performance.

Future research could extend this framework by incorporating contextual variables, exploring alternative spatial representations, and validating entropy-based measures across competitions and tactical environments. More broadly, the results highlight the value of moving beyond aggregate performance metrics toward models that explicitly account for how performance is generated, not only how much is generated.
