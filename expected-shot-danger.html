Expected Shot Danger

Expected Shot Danger (xSD) is a shot-based probability framework designed to capture “how dangerous was this attempt?” without throwing away valuable information from blocked shots. It’s built as a practical alternative to pure xG: closer in spirit to “danger creation” than “goal forecasting,” while still staying probabilistic.

What the model is trying to measure

Traditional expected goals (xG) is excellent at answering “what is the chance this shot becomes a goal?” But football analysis often needs something slightly different: a way to value the danger of an attempt even when it doesn’t become a shot on target — and especially when it gets blocked.

Idea: treat shot danger as a two-step process — first, does the attempt become on target; second, if it’s on target, how likely is it to become a goal?

Why not just use xG?

xG is outcome-focused: it assigns value based on goals. That’s perfect for many use-cases, but it can understate danger in situations where the attempt is prevented (blocked) despite coming from a prime location or context.

  • Blocked shots: often reflect defensive positioning and pressure, not a lack of attacking threat.
  • Danger vs. outcome: a good defense can reduce outcomes while still conceding repeated danger.
  • Link to attacking process: valuing “attempt danger” can complement possession/value models.

Data and setup

The model assumes access to event data with XY locations and shot qualifiers (body part, assist type, technique, play type, deflection flags, etc.). The exact provider doesn’t matter as long as those signals are consistently available.

A key design choice is to treat blocked shots as first-class attempts, rather than discarding them. That’s one of the main ways Expected Shot Danger differs from a standard xG pipeline.

The framework

Expected Shot Danger uses a two-stage probability structure. Conceptually, it mirrors how a goal happens: an attempt must first be directed toward the target, and only then can it beat the goalkeeper.

Expected Shot Danger (xSD) = P(on target) × P(goal | on target)

This creates two complementary views:

  • Pre-shot danger: the chance the attempt becomes on target (accuracy + context).
  • Post-shot danger: the chance an on-target shot becomes a goal (placement/quality vs. keeper).

Features (what goes in)

The model blends contextual qualifiers with physics-style spatial features. In practice, the most useful signals tend to come from:

  • Context: body part, technique (volley/chip/etc.), play type (open play/counter/set piece/penalty), assist type, deflection indicators.
  • Spatial: distance-to-goal, angle-to-goal, and engineered variants of distance/angle.
  • Attempt class: on target / off target / blocked / goal (depending on stage labels).

Training and evaluation

Because probability models can overfit to a single league/season, xSD is best trained on a broad sample across seasons and competitions (where available). Evaluation should focus on ranking quality and calibration, not just raw accuracy.

A common diagnostic is ROC-AUC for each stage (on-target classifier; goal-given-on-target classifier), alongside calibration plots to ensure predicted probabilities behave sensibly across bins.

What you can do with it

Once xSD is computed for every attempt, you can use it the same way you’d use xG — but with extra leverage on situations where teams generate threat that gets denied before a clean shot reaches goal.

  • Team profiles: separate “creates danger” from “turns danger into goals.”
  • Player evaluation: attackers (attempt selection + accuracy) and finishers (post-shot conversion).
  • Blocked-shot analysis: quantify how much danger defenses are actually stopping via blocks.
  • Shot maps: compare pre-shot vs post-shot danger spatially.
